Building software has never been easier. Building the right software is still just as hard.

AI works the same way. Anyone can sign up for a chatbot this afternoon. Getting it to do real work inside your business is a different job.

One tell I look for: if AI in your business mostly means writing emails and summarising meetings, it hasn't made it past the front door. Those jobs are useful. They're also the only ones that need nothing from the business itself: no processes and no data.

In my experience it stalls for four reasons, and none of them are really about the AI.

## Why AI stalls in small businesses

**1 - There's no time to work it out.**
In a team of five or ten, the person who'd set it up is usually the owner. They're also quoting jobs and chasing invoices. Working out AI goes in the "too hard" basket, and it stays there.

**2 - The processes and data aren't set up for it.**
This is the big one. AI can only work from what you give it. If the way you quote a job lives in one person's head, there's nothing to hand over.

Businesses that already have their processes written down can pass them to AI almost as they are. They tend to be the well-run businesses anyway, and they'll be the ones that get the most out of it. Everyone else gets emails and summaries.

Data is the other half. Customer details often sit across a CRM, the accounting software and someone's inbox, with slightly different versions of the truth in each. AI can't see across those tools unless they're connected properly, and connected safely.

**3 - Nobody's sure it's safe.**
Owners are right to ask what happens to client data once it goes into an AI tool. Often nobody in the business knows the answer, so the sensible ones hold off. That caution is fair. It just needs an answer.

**4 - Nobody knows where to start.**
There's always another tool to try. Without a clear first job, the free trial runs out and the subscription keeps billing.

There's a quieter problem sitting under all four. Your team and the business usually want different things from AI. Staff want help with their own day, like a quicker reply or a tidier document. The business wants whole processes simplified. That gap gets overlooked, and it's why plenty of AI rollouts end up as a few personal habits and nothing more.

## What doing AI properly looks like in a small business

The fixes aren't glamorous. Most of the work is manual, and it happens before any AI gets involved.

I've said before that at least 80% of the work is done in setting strategy and building the system. AI hasn't changed that. If anything, it's made it more obvious.

### 1 - Map how the work actually gets done

Pick the jobs that eat your week. For most small businesses that's quotes, follow-ups, onboarding, data entry and reporting.

For each one, write down the steps as they really happen, workarounds included. Note who does it and where it usually gets stuck.

Once a process is written down, you'll often spot a step that doesn't need to exist. That's a saving before AI touches anything.

### 2 - Check your data

You can do this one this week:

- List every tool that holds customer information.
- Pick ten recent customers and check their details match in each tool.
- Check your website forms and CRM are tagged, so you can see where each enquiry came from.
- Look at who has access to what, and whether they still need it.

None of it needs AI. All of it makes AI more useful later, because it's working from the real picture.

### 3 - Get clear on safety first

Before any tool touches client data, you should know where that data goes and who can see it. Check whose account the tool runs on, and read its settings and terms for how it stores and uses what you put in.

If you can't answer that yet, keep client data out of it. Start with a job that doesn't need it.

### 4 - Bring the team with you

People hear "AI" and wonder whether their job is next. Take that seriously.

The point is to take the dull, repetitive parts off their plate, so they get more done and spend more time on the work they actually enjoy. When people see it as help with their own week, they'll tell you where it's needed most.

### 5 - Test one job for a month

Now pick one job and automate it. Note how long it takes before you start, then check again after a month.

If it's working, keep it and move on to the next one. If it isn't, switch it off. A month is long enough to see whether something is real, and short enough that a dud doesn't cost you much.

## What about an AI receptionist for small business?

An AI receptionist is a common place to start, and answering calls and enquiries can be a good first job.

It only works if it knows your answers: what you do, what it costs, how bookings work and when to hand over to a person. If those answers live in your head, you're back at step 1. Write them down first, and the receptionist has something real to work from.

The same goes for most AI tools sold to small businesses. They're only as good as what you feed them.

## How AI can help a small business, once the groundwork is done

With the processes mapped and the data in order, AI stops being a writing assistant and starts doing jobs. Quotes go out faster, and leads get a follow-up without anyone chasing them by hand.

None of that is guaranteed, and it shouldn't be sold that way. Each job either earns its place in a month or gets switched off.
